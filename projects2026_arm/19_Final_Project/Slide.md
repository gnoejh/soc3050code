# Final Project: Build Something Worth Showing
## SOC3050 ARM Edition — Part 3, Applications

**Reference**: [STM32 Reference Manual](https://www.st.com/resource/en/reference_manual/rm0490-stm32c0x1-advanced-armbased-32bit-mcus-stmicroelectronics.pdf) ·
**Board manual**: [UM2953, STM32 Nucleo-64 boards (MB1717)](https://www.st.com/resource/en/user_manual/um2953-stm32c0-nucleo64-board-mb1717-stmicroelectronics.pdf)

**Eighteen lessons of parts. Now one machine — yours.**

Every lesson so far handed you a working program and asked you to take it
apart. This one hands you a **template** that wires every app-board subsystem
together the way a product would — six tasks, three locks, a watchdog that
checks every task is alive, a shell that reports CPU and stack — and leaves
one file empty for you: `app.c`. What goes in it is your project. This deck is
the briefing: what a good one looks like, how it is planned, measured, tested,
demonstrated and graded.

Figures marked **measured on the host** come from `host/run.bat`, which
compiles the template's pure-C half with the PC's gcc and plays it. Nothing in
this deck has been watched running in Wokwi; slide 27 says exactly what that
leaves open.

## The Board: This Lesson's Pins

Before any register, find the pins. Every highlighted dot is a header pin that
this lesson's `diagram.json` wires something to — the same circuit you paste
into Wokwi — at its position on the Nucleo-C031C6, to scale. A label reads
*MCU pin (header name, if different)* and what the pin does here.

The two outer double rows are ST's **morpho** headers, which reach every pin
of the chip; the two inner single rows are the **Arduino**-compatible header
(A0–A5 on the left, D0–D15 on the right). Some MCU pins appear on both — PA5 is
also `D13` — so the highlighted position is the one this lesson's wire uses.
The real board's headers are drawn in its manual, UM2953 (top bar).

`GPIOA->IDR`, bits 2*n*+1 : 2*n* of `GPIOA->MODER`, and so on (lesson 05,
slide 4). Each cell below is one pin, drawn in register order, marked with how
this lesson configures it.

## Slide 1: What You Are About to Build

Six weeks, one person or a pair, one embedded system that does something a
friend would stop and look at — and that **you can prove works**, with numbers.

| You hand in | When |
|---|---|
| a one-page **proposal** with measurable requirements | week 1 |
| a **design review**: task table, timing and memory budgets, test plan | week 2 |
| a **mid demo**: the MVP running, host tests passing | week 4 |
| a **final demo** (live, with an oral) and a **report** | week 6 |

What you start from is this folder. `Main.c`, `shell.c`, `health.c`,
`wdog.c` and `prof.c` are the **platform**; `app.c` is the demo you replace.
`Lab.md` is the handbook — every template and checklist is in it.

## Slide 2: What a Good Embedded Project Looks Like

Not "big". Five properties, and every one is checkable:

| Property | What it means here | How we check |
|---|---|---|
| **does one thing well** | one clear behaviour, finished | the 30-second demo |
| **has numbers** | every requirement has a unit and a limit | report table, `stats` |
| **meets its deadlines** | each task's worst response < its period | `stats` → `over` column = 0 |
| **survives faults** | a hung task, a missing device, a bad input: defined behaviour | fault demo with `hang` |
| **is explained** | you can say why every line is there | the oral |

A small project with all five beats a large one with two. A Snake game
with a measured frame budget, a fuzz test and a watchdog demo outscores a
half-finished 3D maze. The marks are in the five columns, not in the
feature count.

## Slide 9: Requirements Are Numbers

| Vague (rejected) | Measurable (accepted) |
|---|---|
| "the game is smooth" | display ≥ 18 frames/s; app step every 20 ms ± 1 ms |
| "responsive controls" | button press to visible change ≤ 70 ms, worst case |
| "the robot follows the line well" | lap ≤ 12 s; max lateral error ≤ 8 mm, over 20 seeded runs |
| "the drone is stable" | 10° step settles to ±1° in ≤ 0.8 s, overshoot ≤ 15 % |
| "it doesn't crash" | 1 h host fuzz, 0 assertion failures; any hung task → reset ≤ 1.0 s |
| "efficient" | idle ≥ 30 % in `$SYS`; every stack ≤ 75 % of its size |

Each requirement names **what is measured, the unit, the limit, and how** —
host test, `stats`, `$SYS` frame, or a stopwatch on a Wokwi recording. A
requirement you cannot measure is a wish; it goes in "stretch".

The 70 ms latency line is a sum you can do now: input sampled every 10 ms +
app step every 20 ms + display every 50 ms → worst case 10 + 20 + 50 = **80
ms**, plus the I²C flush. So the template as shipped *fails* that line, and
the design review must show how yours meets it.

## Slide 11: Tasks, Rates, Priorities

From the top of `Main.c` — the one table you are expected to edit:

| Task | Period | Prio | Work | Beats? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `input` | 10 ms | 5 | `pad_read()`, sound mailbox, `beep_poll()` | yes |
| `app` | 20 ms | 4 | `app_step(20, &pad)` | yes |
| `telem` | 100 ms | 3 | `$APP` every run, `$SYS` every 10th | yes |
| `display` | 50 ms | 2 | `app_draw()`, `oled_flush()` | yes |
| `health` | 250 ms | 1 | `health_check()`, IWDG refresh, LD4 | — |
| `shell` | on input | 1 | blocks in `uart_getc()` | — |
| `idle` | — | 0 | the kernel's | — |

**Not rate-monotonic, on purpose.** Rate-monotonic order (shorter period →
higher priority) would put `display` (50 ms) above `telem` (100 ms). But a
flush can hold the CPU for ~25 ms of polled I²C, and nothing on a 128×64
screen is harmed by being 5 ms late, while a telemetry line is short and its
timestamps are better on time. **Priorities encode what may wait**, and you
must justify yours in the design review.

`health` is at the **bottom** for the reason slide 16 gives.

## Slide 12: Shared Data — the Sticky-Edge Problem

`input` runs twice per `app` step. If it simply overwrote the shared copy
of the controls, a button press seen on the first run would be gone by the
second — the app would never see it. From `Main.c`:

```c
        /* STICKY EDGES.  Input runs twice per app step.  If it simply
         * overwrote shared_pad, a press seen on the first run would be gone
         * by the second - the app would miss it.  So edges accumulate until
         * the app takes them; levels (stick, knob, down) are just replaced. */
        __disable_irq();
        p.pressed  |= shared_pad.pressed;
        p.released |= shared_pad.released;
        shared_pad  = p;
        __enable_irq();
```

…and `app` copies the struct and clears the edges inside the same kind of
two-line critical section. **Levels are state; edges are events.** State may be
overwritten by a newer value; events must be queued or accumulated until
consumed. Every project hits this somewhere — a "fire" button, a mission
command, a fault flag.

---

## Slide 13: Who Protects What

| Shared thing | Writers → readers | Tool | Why that tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| controls (`pad_t`) | input → app | critical section | 10-byte copy, a few cycles |
| sound request | app → input | one-slot mailbox, flag written last | input is TIM3's only owner |
| app state | app → display, telem | `app_lock` mutex | drawing takes long enough to be preempted |
| I²C1 | display, shell (`scan`) | `bus_lock` mutex | a transfer is ~0.1–25 ms |
| the UART | everyone | `print_lock`, whole lines | lesson 08 |
| heartbeats | each task → health | **one counter per task** | single writer: no lock at all |
| `prof[]` timing | each task → shell | single writer + clear *request* | the shell never writes a counter |

Two rules generate the whole table: **one writer per variable**, where you
can arrange it; and **the cheapest lock that is long enough** where you
cannot — a critical section for a copy, a mutex for anything that waits on
hardware.

Lesson 07's mutex has priority inheritance on (`OS_PRIORITY_INHERIT 1`), so
`display` holding `app_lock` while `app` wants it raises `display` to 4 for
those microseconds rather than letting `telem` preempt it.

## Slide 15: The Independent Watchdog

The IWDG counts down on its own ~32 kHz **LSI** oscillator, not the 48 MHz
system clock; reach zero and it resets the chip. Once started, only a reset
stops it. ST's `HAL_IWDG_Init()` sequence, which `wdog.c` follows:

```regs
# IWDG at 0x40003000, as wdog.c programs it for 1.000 s
KR ; 0xCCCC start, 0x5555 unlock, 0xAAAA refresh | 32 | !15:0 KEY
PR ; /32: 32 kHz -> 1 kHz | 32 = 0x00000003 | !2:0 PR
RLR ; 999 + 1 counts = 1.000 s | 32 = 0x000003E7 | !11:0 RL
SR ; update in progress, polled to 0 | 32 | !2 WVU, !1 RVU, !0 PVU
WINR ; window: never written by wdog.c | 32 | 11:0 WIN
```

| Step | Write | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `KR = 0xCCCC` | start (also switches the LSI on) |
| 2 | `KR = 0x5555` | unlock `PR` and `RLR` |
| 3 | `PR = 3`, `RLR = 999` | 32 000 / 32 = 1000 counts/s; 1000 counts |
| 4 | wait `SR == 0`, **bounded** | values cross into the LSI clock domain |
| 5 | `KR = 0xAAAA` | refresh — and every 250 ms after |

The 32 000 Hz is ST's **typical** value (`LSI_VALUE`, with ST's own note
that it varies with voltage and temperature) — hence refreshing 4× per
timeout, not 1.1×. `wdog.c` also sets `DBG->APBFZ1.DBG_IWDG_STOP`, so a
breakpoint under GDB does not reset the board.

**Wokwi does not implement the IWDG** (its Nucleo-C031C6 page lists it as
not implemented). Slide 17 shows what the template does about that.


**Why counters, not a shared bitmap of "alive" bits?** `seen |= bit` is a
read-modify-write. A task preempted between its load and its store writes
back a stale word and erases the bit a higher-priority task set meanwhile
(lesson 03, slide 7). A counter per task has **one writer**; the bitmap still
exists, as `missing`, built by the one task that owns it.

**Why the lowest priority?** If anything above it spins, `health` starves
too, the refreshes stop, and the chip resets. Feed from a high-priority task
and a runaway lower down would never be noticed.

## Slide 17: Failsafes — Defined Behaviour for Every Fault

| Fault | What the template does | Shown by |
|---|---|---|
| OLED absent (NACK at 0x3C) | banner says so; display skips the flush, everything else runs | banner |
| ADC fails to start | `pad_init()` < 0: buttons still work, stick/knob read 0 | banner |
| a task **hangs** (`hang display`) | its beats stop → `health` prints who, stops refreshing → IWDG reset ≤ 1.0 s | `health`, LD4 stops |
| a task **spins** (`spin input`) | everything below it starves, `health` included → IWDG reset | screen and shell freeze |
| no IWDG (Wokwi) | `health` still alive 1.5 s after the last refresh → `NVIC_SystemReset()` | serial monitor |
| I²C error | counted in `i2c_errors`, reported in `$SYS` and `stats`; `oled.c` keeps the page dirty, so the next flush retries it | `$SYS` |
| bus stuck | `scan` stops at the first timeout; every wait in `i2c.c` is bounded (lesson 09) | `scan` |
| reset of any kind | `RCC->CSR2` read at boot, cause printed, flags cleared with `RMVF` | banner |

```regs
# RCC->CSR2's reset flags - read first thing in main(), then cleared
CSR2 ; reset cause | 32 | !31 LPWRRSTF, !30 WWDGRSTF, !29 IWDGRSTF, !28 SFTRSTF, !27 PWRRSTF, !26 PINRSTF, !25 OBLRSTF, 23 RMVF, 1 LSIRDY, 0 LSION
```

Note the asymmetry the `spin` row teaches: **software cannot rescue software
that has stopped running**. In Wokwi, with no IWDG, `spin input` freezes for
ever — the software fallback lives in the starved task. That is the
argument for a hardware watchdog in one line.

## Slide 18: The Timing Budget

Every task's cost, per second, as a fraction of the CPU or the wire. The
template's own numbers:

| Item | Per run | Rate | Load | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| OLED flush, all 8 pages | 1088 B × 22.5 µs = **24.5 ms** | 20 Hz | **49 % of the I²C bus, polled → CPU** | bytes **measured on the host**; 9 clocks/byte at 400 kHz |
| `$APP` frame | ≤ 35 B on the wire | 10 Hz | 3 % of 115 200 baud | longest body **measured on the host**: 29 B |
| `pad_read()` | 3 ADC conversions, ~21 µs | 100 Hz | ~0.2 % (estimate) | lesson 09: ~7 µs per conversion |
| `app_step()` (steer) | integer only | 50 Hz | < 1 % (estimate) | measure: `stats` → `app` resp |
| context switches | a few µs each | ~400/s | < 1 % (estimate) | `os_switches()` in `$SYS` |

**The display is the budget.** Every frame re-sends all 1088 bytes because
`oled_clear()` dirties every page that had anything on it, and the border and
HUD touch all eight — **measured on the host**, `[6]` in `host/run.bat`.
Lab Part 6 is a contest to cut it.

Your design review fills this table for your project, with *estimate*
written next to every number you have not yet measured — and the final
report replaces each one with a measurement.

## Slide 19: The Memory Budget

From this lesson's build — `arm-none-eabi-size -A Main.elf` and `nm --size-sort`:

| Item | Bytes | |
|---|---|---|
| **FLASH total** | **16 576** | of 32 768 (50.6 %) |
| **RAM total** | **10 280** | of 12 288 (83.7 %) |
| six task stacks | 6 016 | 160 + 256 + 288 + 224 + 256 + 320 words |
| idle stack | 256 | the kernel's |
| OLED framebuffer | 1 032 | 8 × (1 + 128) |
| UART rings | 320 | TX 256 + RX 64 |
| newlib stdio | 388 | `__sf` + `_impure_data` |
| heap + main stack | 1 536 | `link.ld`'s minimum reservation |

**Stacks are the line you will get wrong.** These sizes come from
`-fstack-usage` plus newlib's printf frames read off the disassembly:
`_vfprintf_r` takes 144 bytes, `_printf_i` 64, and the chain from `say()` to
the UART ring ~650 bytes. **Any task that prints needs ~650 bytes for printf
alone.** A static estimate, not a measurement — `stats` prints the real
high-water mark (lesson 07's `os_stack_used()`). Requirement: every stack
≤ 75 % used after the full demo.

2 KB of RAM is left. A second 1 KB framebuffer would not fit; a 64-entry
replay log would.

## Slide 20: Measuring on the Target — `stats`

```
task     pri  cpu%   stack used/size   period  runs  resp avg/max us  late  over
```

| Column | Comes from | Exact? |
|---|---|---|
| `cpu%` | the kernel's per-task tick count (`os_task_t.ticks`) | **sampled** at 1 kHz — fair over seconds |
| `stack used/size` | `os_stack_used()`: untouched `0xDEADBEEF` words | exact high-water mark |
| `resp avg/max` | TIM14 at 1 MHz, wake → done | exact; **includes preemption** |
| `late` | `os_ticks()` at wake − due tick | ms resolution |
| `over` | runs whose response exceeded the period | a missed deadline |

`stats` zeroes the timing counters each time, so two `stats` a few seconds
apart measure exactly that interval — play hard between them.

`resp` is **response time**, not execution time: it includes every
higher-priority task that ran in between. That is the number a deadline
cares about. `cpu%` is the other half — what the task itself consumed.

TIM14 because it is free: TIM3 is the buzzer's, SysTick the kernel's.
48 MHz / (`PSC` 47 + 1) = 1 MHz; a 16-bit difference is correct across one
wrap, so spans up to 65 ms measure exactly.

## Slide 21: The Test Plan, Part 1 — Host Tests

`host/test_app.c` is a **harness template**: copy it, keep the pattern.

1. **a fake platform** — `app_sound()` and `i2c_write_read()` stubs that count;
2. **scripted inputs** — a `pad_t` per step, or a *bot* computed from state;

## Slide 26: AI Tools — Allowed, Disclosed, Understood

You may use AI assistants (code completion, chat models) for this project.
Professionals do. Three conditions:

1. **Disclose.** The report has an "AI use" section: which tool, for what
   (e.g. "drafted the A* open-list code", "explained the IWDG sequence"),
   and what you changed. No disclosure + evident AI code = an integrity case.
2. **Understand every line.** The oral picks lines from your repository at
   random. "The AI wrote that" is not an answer; for a line you cannot
   explain, that part of the grade is zero, and repeated cases are an
   integrity case.
3. **Verify, don't trust.** AI tools invent register names and bit positions
   with full confidence. Every register you use is grepped in
   `stm32c031xx.h` and listed in your report's "Facts checked" table — the
   same rule every lesson in this course followed.

Not allowed: submitting another team's code, or code from a previous
year's project, with or without AI in between.
