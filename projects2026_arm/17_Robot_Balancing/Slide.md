# Robot Balancing: PID vs LQR
## SOC3050 ARM Edition — Part 3, Applications

**Reference**: [STM32 Reference Manual](https://www.st.com/resource/en/reference_manual/rm0490-stm32c0x1-advanced-armbased-32bit-mcus-stmicroelectronics.pdf) ·
**Board manual**: [UM2953, STM32 Nucleo-64 boards (MB1717)](https://www.st.com/resource/en/user_manual/um2953-stm32c0-nucleo64-board-mb1717-stmicroelectronics.pdf)

**A robot that stands on two wheels — inside a chip with no robot attached.**

Push it and it catches itself. Load it and it copes, up to a point. Change the
controller with one button and watch the same robot either hold its spot,
wander off across the room, or fall on its face. The robot is a physics model
running at 1 kHz on the Nucleo; the controller beside it sees only a gyro, an
accelerometer and an encoder, exactly as a real one would.

Three controllers compete: **PID on tilt**, **cascaded PID**, and **LQR** —
the one whose gains are *computed* by a 300-line Python script from the same
numbers the simulation uses. Every figure marked **measured on the host** comes
from `host/run.sh`, which runs this lesson's own `world.c` and `control.c` on a
PC. None of this has been watched running in Wokwi yet.

## The Board: This Lesson's Pins

Before any register, find the pins. Every highlighted dot is a header pin that
this lesson's `diagram.json` wires something to — the same circuit you paste
into Wokwi — at its position on the Nucleo-C031C6, to scale. A label reads
*MCU pin (header name, if different)* and what the pin does here.

The two outer double rows are ST's **morpho** headers, which reach every pin
of the chip; the two inner single rows are the **Arduino**-compatible header
(A0–A5 on the left, D0–D15 on the right). Some MCU pins appear on both — PA5 is
also `D13` — so the highlighted position is the one this lesson's wire uses.
The real board's headers are drawn in its manual, UM2953 (top bar).

`GPIOA->IDR`, bits 2*n*+1 : 2*n* of `GPIOA->MODER`, and so on (lesson 05,
slide 4). Each cell below is one pin, drawn in register order, marked with how

## Slide 2: The Contract — `control.h`

```c
typedef struct {
    float   gyro;      /* pitch rate from the IMU's gyro, rad/s - with bias and noise  */
    float   acc_f;     /* accelerometer, along the body's forward axis, m/s^2          */
    float   acc_u;     /* along the body's up axis, m/s^2                              */
```

## Slide 5: The Motor Fights Back

A DC motor is a voltage source driving its winding resistance against its own
back-EMF:

```c
float w_rel = w->xd * (1.0f / BOT_R) - w->thd;     /* wheel speed RELATIVE to the body */
float tau = MOT_N * (MOT_K * (u - MOT_K * w_rel) / MOT_RES - MOT_B * w_rel);
```

| | per motor | both |
|---|---|---|
| stall torque, 7.4 V | k V / R = 0.37 N m | 0.74 N m → 21 N at the tyre |
| no-load speed | V / k = 37 rad/s | 1.3 m/s |

Two facts every controller must live with:

- **A volt buys less torque the faster the wheels already turn.** At 1 m/s
  only 1.7 V of the 7.4 are left to push with.
- **u can never exceed 7.4 V.** `world_step()` clips it and counts the clipped
  steps; `stats` reports them.

The motor is bolted to the body, so its speed is the wheel's *relative to the
body* — the same reason the encoder reads relative angle (slide 9).

## Slide 6: 1 kHz, With No FPU — Semi-Implicit Euler

```c
w->xdd  = (a22 * b1 - a12 * b2) / det;     /* solve the 2x2 system      */
w->thdd = (a11 * b2 - a12 * b1) / det;

w->xd  += w->xdd  * WORLD_DT;              /* speeds first...            */
w->thd += w->thdd * WORLD_DT;
w->x   += w->xd   * WORLD_DT;              /* ...positions from the NEW  */
w->th  += w->thd  * WORLD_DT;              /*    speeds                  */
```

Plain Euler (positions from the *old* speeds) adds a little energy every step:
an undriven pendulum swings higher and higher. Swapping two lines fixes that
— the *symplectic* ordering.

**Why not RK2?** It evaluates the equations twice per step. On a Cortex-M0+
every `float` add and multiply is a library call (lesson 10), so RK2 doubles
the world's CPU time for accuracy we do not need: the fastest motion here has
a time constant of ~14 ms (slide 7), fourteen steps long.

`f_sin`/`f_cos` from `fmath.h` replace `sinf`/`cosf` — a dozen multiplies
instead of ~4 KB of flash for range reduction we never use.

## Slide 7: Linearised — What `lqr.py` Sees

About upright, `sin θ ≈ θ`, `cos θ ≈ 1`, `θ'² ≈ 0`, and the equations become
`x' = A x + B u` with **x = [x, ẋ, θ, θ̇]** and **u = volts**.
`lqr.py` derives A and B from `params.h` — **measured on the host**:

```

  [    0.0000   -54.8639   -11.4674     1.9162 ]   [   7.9843 ]
```

Four poles, four stories:

|---|---|
| **+7.04** | **falling over**: a tilt multiplies by e every 142 ms |
| 0 | position: nothing in physics cares where the robot is |

| −70.4 | the motor's own fast electrical-mechanical damping |

The controller's period, 5 ms, is 28 times shorter than 142 ms. That ratio
— comfortably above 10 — is why 200 Hz is enough.

## Slide 8: Sensors That Lie

| sensor | what it measures | how it lies |
|---|---|---|
| **gyro** | θ̇, the tilt *rate* | + 0.005 rad/s bias, + noise. Integrate it and the bias grows into an error **forever** |
| **accelerometer** | specific force: acceleration *minus gravity*, in body axes | noise — and it **cannot tell leaning from accelerating** |
| **encoder** | wheel angle *relative to the body*, in whole counts | quantised: 1 count = 0.15 mm of travel |

The accelerometer's trap, from `world_sense()`:

```c
float af = fx * c - fy * s;          /* fx = x'' + ..., fy = g + ...       */
```

Standing still, `atan2(-af, au)` is the tilt. Accelerating forward at *a*,
it reads the tilt **minus about a/g** — so a robot that is busy catching itself
looks like it is leaning the other way. And the IMU is mounted 1° crooked,
which no filter can see.

## Slide 10: Where Am I? Encoder + Tilt

```c
float wheel = (float)in->enc * (F_2PI / ENC_CPR) + est.th;   /* relative + tilt */
est.x = BOT_R * wheel;
float raw = (est.x - x_prev) * (1.0f / CTRL_DT);
est.xd += XD_FILTER * (raw - est.xd);                         /* low-pass       */
```

The encoder lives on the motor, the motor on the body. Tilt the body forward
with the wheels held still and the encoder *counts* — so the tilt must be
added back to get the wheel's turn over the ground.

Speed by differencing is lumpy: one count in 5 ms is 0.03 m/s. A first-order
low-pass (0.25 per step, ~17 ms) smooths it, at the price of a little lag —
lag that every speed loop downstream inherits.

## Slide 11: Three Controllers

| | sees | output | gains from |
|---|---|---|---|
| **PID tilt** | θ, ∫θ, θ̇ | `u = KP θ + KI ∫θ + KD θ̇` | hand-tuned |
| **cascade** | x, ẋ → a lean; θ, θ̇ → volts | two PDs, one inside the other | hand-tuned |
| **LQR** | [x, ẋ, θ, θ̇] all at once | `u = −K x` | **computed** by `lqr.py` |

All three run the same estimator, the same command slew (the joystick's
speed command ramps at 0.5 m/s²), and the same failsafe:

```c
if (est.th > TRIP_ANGLE || est.th < -TRIP_ANGLE) {   /* 50 degrees */
    integ = 0.0f;
    out->volts = 0.0f;
    return;
}
```

A robot lying on the floor with its wheels at full power is how people get
hurt. The failsafe comes **before** any control law, so no mode can skip it.

## Slide 12: PID on Tilt — It Stands. Then It Leaves.

```c
integ = f_clamp(integ + est.th * CTRL_DT, -0.1f, 0.1f);
u = TILT_KP * est.th + TILT_KI * integ + TILT_KD * in->gyro;   /* 150, 50, 4 */
```

The obvious controller: tilt forward, drive forward. Nothing in it knows
where the wheels are.

**Measured on the host**, from upright, no command: **it balances — while
rolling away — and falls after 7.8 s, 3.3 m from where it started.**

Why it *must* fail, in one chain:

1. To keep rolling at speed v, the motor needs voltage against its back-EMF.
2. This controller only makes voltage when the robot is **tilted**.
3. A tilted robot must **accelerate** to keep from falling.
4. So speed grows, needing more voltage, needing more tilt — until the motor
   runs out of volts (slide 5) and the robot goes over.

And the IMU is 1° crooked: "tilt = 0" by its reckoning is a 1° lean in truth,
which by itself demands a steady acceleration.

## Slide 13: The Eigenvalues Agree

`lqr.py --pid KP,KI,KD` closes the tilt-only loop around the *linear* model
and prints its eigenvalues — **measured on the host**:

```
$ python lqr.py --pid 150,0,4
  +1.00152+0.00000j   |z| = 1.00152   UNSTABLE: grows e-fold every 3.29 s
$ python lqr.py --pid 25,0,2
  +1.00847-0.00000j   |z| = 1.00847   UNSTABLE: grows e-fold every 0.59 s
$ python lqr.py --pid 150,50,4
  +1.00000+0.00000j   |z| = 1.00000   = 1: position - nothing pulls it back (drift)
  +0.99992-0.00035j   |z| = 0.99992   slow swing, period 88.9 s
```

- **PD alone runs away** — slower the higher KP is (step 4 of slide 12 needs
  less tilt per volt), but always.
- **Adding I** turns the run-away into a swing with an 89-second period, just
  inside the unit circle. ∫θ acts like a speed sensor in disguise — a robot
  that has leaned forward for a while has gained speed.
- Either way there is an eigenvalue at exactly 1: **position**. Nothing
  measures it, so nothing restores it.

An 89 s swing is stable on paper and useless in a room: any offset drives it
on a journey longer than the motor can follow. That is the gap between
"stable" and "good".


To go forward you must first lean forward — so the outer loop chooses a
*lean*, and the inner loop holds it. Tune the inner loop first, then the outer
one several times slower.

**The clamp was a lesson of its own.** At ±0.10 rad (6°) the robot **fell**
when asked for 0.3 m/s (**measured on the host**): the outer loop wanted more
lean than it was allowed, the target position kept moving, the error piled up
— windup through the set-point — and it overshot to 1 m/s and ran out of volts.
At ±0.30 rad it drives fine. A limit inside a loop is a nonlinearity, and
nonlinearities bite exactly when you need the loop most.

## Slide 15: The Cascade Is State Feedback in Disguise

Multiply the cascade out:

```
u = KP·θ + KD·θ̇ + KP·KPX·(x − x_ref) + KP·KVX·(ẋ − v_ref)      (inside the clamp)
  = 25·θ + 2·θ̇ + 5.0·(x − x_ref) + 15·(ẋ − v_ref)
```

Compare LQR's gain, `u = −K x`:

| | x (V/m) | ẋ (V per m/s) | θ (V/rad) | θ̇ (V per rad/s) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| cascade | 5.0 | 15.0 | 25.0 | 2.0 |
| **LQR** | **5.12** | **15.26** | **21.26** | **2.46** |

Same four numbers, near enough. That is no accident: the cascade's outer
gains here were chosen with the inner gain fixed so that the products came out
LQR-sized. The real difference is **how you get the four numbers** — by
trial, one loop at a time, or by a calculation that weighs them all at once —
and what happens at the clamp.

## Slide 16: LQR — Say What You Want, Get the Gains

The **linear quadratic regulator** finds the K that minimises

```
J = Σ  ( xᵀ Q x  +  u R u )        over all future steps
       k
Q = diag(q_x, q_v, q_θ, q_ω)       how much each error HURTS
R                                   how much each volt COSTS
```

| raise | and the robot... |
|---|---|
| `q_x` | holds its spot harder — leans harder to get back |
| `q_θ` | stands straighter — spends volts to do it |
| `q_ω` | moves its tilt more gently |
| `R` | is lazier with the motor: softer, smaller voltages, more wander |

Only **ratios** matter: doubling Q and R together gives the same K. Start
with each weight at 1/(largest acceptable error)² — Bryson's rule — and
adjust by watching.

The default: `Q = diag(30, 2, 50, 0.5)`, `R = 1` in `params.h`.

---

## Slide 17: The Riccati Equation, by Iteration

`lqr.py`, standard library only, in four steps:

```python
A, B   = linear_model(p)              # slide 7, from params.h
Ad, Bd = discretise(A, B, dt)         # zero-order hold: exp([[A, B], [0, 0]] dt)
for k in range(iters):                # P <- Q + A'PA - A'PB (R + B'PB)^-1 B'PA
    ...
K = (R + B'PB)^-1 B'PA
```

- **Discretise first.** The controller holds u for 5 ms; designing for the
  continuous model would ignore that. `expm()` is a Taylor series with
  scaling and squaring — 20 lines.
- **Iterate the Riccati equation** until P stops changing. One input means
  `R + B'PB` is a number, so the "inverse" is a division. It converged in
  **2729 iterations** (measured on the host).
- **Check it** (slide 18), then print a line of C.

No numpy. The matrix helpers — `mul`, `add`, `tr`, `expm` — are each a line or
two, so every step is readable.

## Slide 18: The Numbers — and the Proof It Stabilises

**Measured on the host**, `python host/lqr.py`:

```
K = [ -5.1239  -15.2554  -21.2619  -2.4627 ]    u = -K x
  per unit:  -5.1 V per m,  -15.26 V per m/s,  -0.371 V per degree,  -0.0430 V per deg/s

closed-loop eigenvalues of Ad - Bd K (|z| < 1 is stable):
  +0.99606-0.00000j   |z| = 0.99606   time constant 1268.0 ms
  +0.97958+0.00000j   |z| = 0.97958   time constant  242.4 ms
  +0.95914+0.00000j   |z| = 0.95914   time constant  119.9 ms
  +0.66211+0.00000j   |z| = 0.66211   time constant   12.1 ms
spectral radius 0.99606  (power check ||Acl^2000||^(1/2000) = 0.99655)
linear model from 6 degrees: peak |u| 2.23 V (within the 7.4 V supply), settled ... after 2.10 s

paste into control.c:
  const float LQR_K[4] = { -5.1239f, -15.255f, -21.262f, -2.4627f };
```

Eigenvalues come from the characteristic polynomial (Faddeev–LeVerrier) and
its roots (Durand–Kerner); `‖A^2000‖^(1/2000)` checks them independently.
All four inside the unit circle: **stable**. The slow one, 1.27 s, is the
robot creeping back to its spot.

`host/run.sh` runs `lqr.py --check ../control.c`: if someone edits
`params.h` and forgets to paste the new K, **the test fails**.

host**, noise on:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30, 2, 50, 0.5 | 1 | −5.1, −15.3, −21.3, −2.5 | 3.00 s | 0.78 N s | 1.36 kg |
| **300**, 2, 50, 0.5 | 1 | −16.1, −19.0, −24.9, −3.0 | 6.18 s | 0.58 | 0.80 |

The intuition holds and then surprises:

- Caring hugely about **position** (`q_x` = 300) made it *worse* at

## Slide 20: The Firmware — Five Tasks, One Owner Each

| task | prio | period | does | stack |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `world` | 5 | 1 ms | apply requests, `world_step`, `world_sense`, publish | 160 words |
| `control` | 4 | 5 ms | copy sensors, `control_step`, publish volts | 160 |
| `ui` | 3 | 20 ms | `pad_read`, buzzer, `$BAL` every 100 ms | 384 |
| `shell` | 2 | on input | `stats mode noise alpha vbat push k` | 384 |
| `display` | 1 | 100 ms | `view_draw`, `oled_flush` over I²C | 384 |

**One writer per variable.** `world_t` belongs to the world task alone; the
others *ask* through `req_standup`, `req_push`, `req_payload`, `req_noise`,
`req_vbat`, read at the top of each world step. Even resetting the controller
after a stand-up is a request (`req_reset`) that the control task carries out
on itself — so it can never be reset half-way through a step.

Multi-field snapshots cross tasks inside a two-line critical section:

```c
__disable_irq();
snap = s;          /* sensors_t: gyro, accel, encoder must be from ONE instant */
truth = tr;
__enable_irq();
```

`cmd_volts` is one aligned 32-bit `float`: a single store, no tearing.

## Slide 21: Timing It — TIM14 as a Stopwatch

The M0+ has **no cycle counter** — the DWT's `CYCCNT` exists on the M3 and up,
not here. A spare timer counting microseconds does the job:

```c
RCC->APBENR2 |= RCC_APBENR2_TIM14EN;
TIM14->PSC = (uint16_t)(SystemCoreClock / 1000000u - 1u);   /* 48 MHz / 48 = 1 MHz */
TIM14->ARR = 0xFFFFu;
TIM14->EGR = TIM_EGR_UG;                                     /* load PSC now        */
TIM14->CR1 = TIM_CR1_CEN;
```

```regs
RCC->APBENR2 | 32 | 0, 11, 12, 14, !15 TIM14EN, 17, 18, 20
TIM14->PSC ; 48 MHz / (47 + 1) = 1 MHz | 16 = 0x002F | !15:0 PSC
TIM14->EGR ; UG: copy PSC into the counter now | 16 = 0x0001 | !0 UG
TIM14->CR1 | 16 = 0x0001 | 7 ARPE, !0 CEN
```

`EGR.UG` matters: `PSC` is buffered and only takes effect at the next update
event — without it the first 65 ms count at 48 MHz. Each task reads
`TIM14->CNT` before and after its work; 16-bit unsigned subtraction survives
the wrap. `stats` prints the results.

## Slide 22: The CPU Budget — Estimated, Not Yet Measured

Counting the float operations in the source and pricing each at ~70 cycles
(soft-float on the M0+, lesson 10's order of magnitude):

| work | float ops (approx.) | per call | rate | CPU |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `world_step` + `world_sense` | ~165 incl. 4 `f_sin`/`f_cos`, 3 noise draws | ~240 µs | 1000/s | **~25 %** |
| `control_step` | ~40 + one `f_atan2` divide | ~100 µs | 200/s | ~2 % |
| `view_draw` + `oled_flush` | integer drawing; I²C ~25 ms for a full frame | ms | 10/s | the rest, at the lowest priority |

**This is an estimate.** Nothing in this repository can time Cortex-M0+
instructions — the host test runs on an x86. The first thing to do in Wokwi is
type `stats`, which prints the measured µs per step from TIM14 and the share
of CPU it adds up to. If the world takes more than a millisecond, the kernel
cannot keep its 1 kHz and the physics slows down — the robot would fall in
slow motion, and `stats` would show it.

The host meanwhile proves the run is **deterministic**: two runs with the same
seed agree bit for bit (measured on the host).

## Slide 25: The Contest — Measured on the Host

`host/run.sh`: the firmware's own `world.c` and `control.c`, noise on,
seed 12345.

| | settle from 6° | peak V | largest push | 30 s, no command | payload (0.1 N s push) | drive 0.3 m/s, 5 s |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PID tilt | never | 7.40 | 0.62 N s | **fell at 7.8 s, 3.3 m away** | 0.16 kg | went **backwards** 1.71 m |
| cascade | **1.70 s** | 2.10 | 0.60 N s | 9 cm off | **1.88 kg** | 1.72 m |
| **LQR** | 3.00 s | **1.80** | **0.78 N s** | 7 cm off | 1.36 kg | 1.72 m |

Read honestly:

- **PID on tilt cannot do the job**, as slides 12–13 predicted.
- **LQR takes the biggest shove** — 30 % more than the cascade — with the
  least voltage, and its gains came from a calculation, not an afternoon.
- **The cascade settles faster and carries more.** Its tilt gain is a little
  stiffer (25 vs 21 V/rad), which plausibly helps with a top-heavy load — a
  guess, not something these runs isolate. LQR does not win every column with
  the default Q; slide 19 shows a Q that does.
- Both ran **ahead** of a moving target (1.72 m vs the target's 1.41 m): only
  a position error produces the voltage the back-EMF demands. Lab Part 8 adds
  one line of feed-forward and closes the gap to zero (measured).

## Slide 27: What Carries Forward

- **The SITL boundary.** `control_step(const sensors_t*, actuators_t*)` is the
  seam between "the robot" and "the brain". Lesson 18's sumo robots fight
  across the same seam; anything on the far side can be a model or a motor.
- **Model → linearise → design → verify.** `lqr.py` is the whole of a control
  engineer's workflow in 300 lines: one source of parameters, a computed gain,
  a proof it is stable, and a test that fails when the two drift apart.
- **Stable is not good.** An 89-second eigenvalue inside the unit circle still
  rolled the robot 3 m into a wall.
- **Estimation before control.** Every controller here is only as good as
  θ_est; three of the four lab failures are estimator failures.
- **The real-time budget.** A 1 kHz physics task, a 200 Hz controller and a
  slow I²C display, sharing one no-FPU core, ordered by priority and measured
  by a timer.
