Drone Missions: Where to Go, and How to Come Home

SOC3050 ARM Edition — Part 3, Applications

Reference: STM32 Reference Manual ·
Board manual: UM2953, STM32 Nucleo-64 boards (MB1717) ·
Protocol: MAVLink mission protocol

Press A. A drone takes off, flies five waypoints in a gale, comes home and
lands on the spot it left — and if you push it past the fence or drain its
battery, it decides to come home on its own.

Lesson 13 kept a drone level. This lesson makes it go somewhere: position
hold, missions sent from Python, and the failsafes every real autopilot has.
There is no drone in Wokwi, so the firmware carries one — a simulated aircraft
in its own RTOS task, seen by the flight computer only through noisy, late
sensors.

Every number marked measured on the host comes from host/run.sh, which
compiles this lesson's own world.c, flight.c, mavlite.c and ui.c with
the PC's gcc and flies them. Nothing in this deck was watched in Wokwi.

The Board: This Lesson's Pins

Before any register, find the pins. Every highlighted dot is a header pin that
this lesson's diagram.json wires something to — the same circuit you paste
into Wokwi — at its position on the Nucleo-C031C6, to scale. A label reads
MCU pin (header name, if different) and what the pin does here.

The two outer double rows are ST's morpho headers, which reach every pin
of the chip; the two inner single rows are the Arduino-compatible header
(A0–A5 on the left, D0–D15 on the right). Some MCU pins appear on both — PA5 is
also D13 — so the highlighted position is the one this lesson's wire uses.
The real board's headers are drawn in its manual, UM2953 (top bar).

The Chip: The Same Pins, as Port Bits

Inside the chip the header positions disappear: every pin is one bit
position in its port's registers. Pin n of GPIOA is bit n of
GPIOA->IDR, bits 2*n*+1 : 2*n* of GPIOA->MODER, and so on (lesson 05,
slide 4). Each cell below is one pin, drawn in register order, marked with how
this lesson configures it.

Slide 2: The Whole Machine

One contract crosses the line between plant and controller — control.h:

void control_step(const sensors_t *in, actuators_t *out);

sensors_t carries what a flight controller really gets: acceleration, a
compass, a barometer, a GPS fix that is 200 ms old. actuators_t carries what
it really commands: tilt, thrust, yaw rate. Lesson 13 implements the same
function one level down.

Slide 3: Why We May Pretend the Attitude Loop Is Instant

Lesson 13's loops turn "tilt 10° right" into four motor speeds, hundreds of
times a second. This lesson does not simulate them. It replaces all of
lesson 13 with one line per axis:

w->roll += (r_cmd - w->roll) * dt / w->tau_att;      /* world.c: a first-order lag, 80 ms */

That is legitimate when the inner loop is much faster than the outer one —
time-scale separation. Seen from a loop that reacts in seconds, a loop that
settles in 80 ms is "already there". Every cascaded controller you will meet
rests on that argument; here it is measured, by slowing the inner loop
down (TAU) and holding position in an 8 m/s wind:

Slide 4: The Model — a Thrust Vector, Tilted

A multirotor can only push along its own axis. To move sideways it must
tilt, and the price of tilting is lift: tip 25° and the vertical share of
thrust falls by 1 - cos 25° = 9%, which the altitude loop must make up.

Drag is linear in airspeed — rotor drag, the dominant term for a small
multirotor at these speeds — and it acts on velocity relative to the air.
That one choice is where the wind enters the physics: hovering in an 8 m/s
wind costs 0.35 × 8 = 2.8 m/s² of permanent sideways push.

Slide 5: Wind You Can Repeat

The wind is a steady part (the knob, 0–10 m/s) plus gusts: each axis an
Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process — decays toward zero over 2 s, kicked by noise,
settling at 30% of the steady wind.

float kick = sigma * m_sqrt(2.0f * dt / GUST_TAU);
w->gust_x += -w->gust_x * dt / GUST_TAU + kick * gauss(w);

The noise comes from xorshift32 — three shifts, three XORs, period
2³² − 1 — and Gaussians from summing three uniforms (central limit theorem: no
logf, no sqrtf, cheap with no FPU):

x ^= x << 13;  x ^= x >> 17;  x ^= x << 5;

Deterministic on purpose. One seed is one exact storm, on the board and on
the PC. Measured on the host: the same mission flown twice with seed 1 in
8 m/s wind gives a bit-identical flight. So when your change moves a
number, your change did it — not luck.

The GPS error that matters is the slow wander: real receivers drift by
metres over seconds as the satellites move. Measured on the host: the
modelled GPS is off by 0.65 m RMS — and no filter on board can remove an
error that every measurement shares. Hold that thought until slide 14.

The accelerometer here is already in the world frame, gravity removed:
that is what lesson 13's attitude estimate buys you. Rotating a body-frame
reading by the attitude is lesson 13's job; using it is this one's.

Slide 8: Estimation — Predict, Then Correct

Neither sensor alone will do: the accelerometer is fast but drifts (a 0.03
m/s² bias is 1.5 m off after 10 s); the GPS does not drift but is slow,
late and jumpy. So use both:

/* predict - every 20 ms */
fc.vx += s->acc_x * dt;
fc.px += fc.vx * dt;
/* correct - every 100 ms, when a fix arrives:  r = how wrong we were */
fc.px += a * rx;                  /* alpha = 0.25 */
fc.vx += b * rx;                  /* beta  = 0.04 / 0.1 s */

An alpha-beta filter with the accelerometer as its model — the fixed-gain
cousin of the Kalman filter real autopilots run. alpha decides how much of
each surprise goes into the position, beta how much into the velocity. Both
are $PARAMs (EST_A, EST_B): turn them up and the estimate follows the
GPS's jitter; turn them down and it follows the accelerometer's drift.

Slide 10: Altitude — a Complementary Filter

The barometer has no delay here, so altitude gets the same idea in its simplest
form: trust the accelerometer for fast changes and the baro for the average.

float r = s->baro_alt - fc.pz;
fc.vz += (s->acc_z + 4.0f * r) * dt;          /* omega^2  = 4    */
fc.pz += (fc.vz    + 2.8f * r) * dt;          /* 2 zeta w = 2.8  */

That is a second-order loop pulling the estimate toward the baro with natural
frequency 2 rad/s and damping 0.7: baro noise above ~0.3 Hz is filtered out,
accelerometer drift below it is corrected.

Measured on the host: touchdowns at 0.41–0.43 m/s on every normal
landing — the landing logic (slide 15) trusts this estimate to know when it is
low enough to slow down, and when it has stopped.

Slide 11: Cascaded Control

Each block has one job and one limit: position error becomes a velocity no
faster than HOLD_V or CRUISE; velocity error becomes an acceleration no
larger than g tan(TILT) = 4.57 m/s² at 25°. The limits are what make a
cascade safe: no position error, however large, can command more than a
25° lean.

The bandwidths fall outward — 12.5, ~2.4, ~1 rad/s — slide 3's separation at
every step.

Slide 12: From Acceleration to Tilt

Quoted from control_step():

float cy = m_cos(fc.yaw), sy = m_sin(fc.yaw);
float a_fwd   = ax * cy + ay * sy;               /* world -> the drone's own axes */
float a_right = ax * sy - ay * cy;
float a_up    = GRAVITY + az;
o->thrust   = m_sqrt(a_fwd * a_fwd + a_right * a_right + a_up * a_up);
o->pitch    = m_atan2(a_fwd, a_up);
o->roll     = m_atan2(a_right, m_sqrt(a_fwd * a_fwd + a_up * a_up));

This is slide 4 run backwards: "I want this acceleration" → "point the thrust
vector there, this hard". The drone is yawed to face along each leg, so the
same world acceleration becomes a different pitch/roll mix as it turns — the
rotation by yaw does that bookkeeping.

m_sin, m_atan2, m_sqrt are fmath.h's functions compiled once
(fm.c) — slide 21 explains the m_.

Slide 13: The Integral Term Is a Wind Gauge

In a steady 8 m/s wind, holding still means a permanent lean into it. A
proportional controller can only lean if there is an error — so without an
integral the drone settles downwind. Measured on the host, 30 s hold:

mean offset downwindtrue RMS error
KI_VEL = 0.6 (default)0.23 m1.01 m
KI_VEL = 01.26 m1.59 m

The prediction for the second row is a line of algebra: the drag to cancel is
0.35 × 8 = 2.8 m/s², and two proportional gains in series give
2.8 / (1.0 × 2.0) = 1.4 m. Measured 1.26 m — gusts and the sampled
estimate account for the rest.

With the integral, KI_VEL × ix is the 2.8 m/s² — so dividing by the drag
coefficient reads the wind: measured 7.1 m/s against a true steady
8.0 m/s, and the tilt into it 12.9°. Real autopilots estimate wind exactly
this way when they have no airspeed sensor.

Slide 14: You Cannot Hold Better Than You Can See

Position hold, 8 m/s wind with gusts, 30 s — the error split three ways
(measured on the host):

The drone reports the seen error and is judged on the true one. No
gain on board can touch the blind part: it is the GPS's own wander (0.65 m,
slide 7), shared by every fix. That is why survey drones carry RTK GPS, and why
Lab Part 3's target is set on the error you can move.

Slide 15: Flight Modes — One State Machine

Every change goes through one function, enter(), which logs it (an
$EVT frame, with a reason) and gives the new mode a defined setpoint —
"stay here" — never whatever the last mode left behind. RTL climbs to at least
10 m before it travels (it never descends to go home), then flies home, then
lands on home rather than wherever it happened to stop.

Slide 16: Following a Line, Not Chasing a Point

float along  = ex * tx + ey * ty;                       /* toward B, along the leg */
float across = ex * -ty + ey * tx;                      /* off the line            */
float va = clampf(fc.p.kp_pos * along,  fc.p.cruise);   /* limited to cruise       */
float vc = clampf(fc.p.kp_pos * across, fc.p.cruise);   /* its own full correction */

Aiming at B limits the whole velocity vector to the cruise speed, so the
correction toward the line is starved while the drone is far from B. Splitting
the error gives the cross-track part its own budget. Measured on the host,
8 m/s wind: max cross-track 2.07 m (line) against 2.34 m (aim), RMS
1.00 against 1.13 m, for 0.2 s more flight time.

Slide 17: Waypoints, and What "Reached" Means

A waypoint counts as reached when the estimate is within ACCEPT (1 m)
horizontally and 0.7 m vertically. The next leg then starts at the
waypoint, not at wherever the drone was — so the planned path, not the
flown one, is what the cross-track error is measured against.

Measured on the host, closest true approach to each of the five waypoints
(m):

Waypoint 3 is missed by 1.7 m in both — so it is not the wind. It is
accepted at 1 m by an estimate that is ~0.7 m off at that moment: acceptance
radius plus blind error. Shrink ACCEPT and you pass closer, slower — the
trade Lab Part 7's race is about.

Slide 18: Failsafes Outrank Every Command

Quoted from flight.c, run every 20 ms before the mode logic:

if (fc.battery < fc.p.bat_crit && m != M_LAND) {
    enter(M_LAND, R_BATTERY_CRIT);                       /* no time to get home */
    return;
}
if (m == M_TAKEOFF || m == M_HOLD || m == M_MISSION) {
    if (fc.battery < fc.p.bat_low) { enter(M_RTL, R_BATTERY); return; }
    float r2 = fc.px * fc.px + fc.py * fc.py;
    if (r2 > fc.p.fence_r * fc.p.fence_r || fc.pz > fc.p.fence_alt) {
        enter(M_RTL, R_FENCE);
    }
}

Slide 20: MAVLink-lite

Real MAVLink is binary with a CRC-16; its mission upload is a handshake in
which the vehicle asks for each item (MISSION_REQUEST_INT). This lesson
keeps lesson 08's printable frame and the same ideas:

Positions are integers in decimetres from home — no floats on the wire.
MAVLink does the same: MISSION_ITEM_INT sends local positions as
metres × 10⁴ in an int32.

Our upload has no handshake: the host waits for each $ACK and resends up
to three times (mission.py --port). Simpler, and fine on a wire that rarely
drops a line — Lab Part 8 asks what it would cost on a radio that does.

Slide 21: A Parser That Trusts Nothing

ml_handle() checks in this order, and refuses at the first failure:

  1. the checksum (lesson 08's proto_check) → ERR,CHECKSUM
  2. the length — a body over 63 bytes is refused, never truncated
  3. the field count for that command
  4. every number, strictly: "12abc" is not 12
  5. the range: index 0–15, ±1 km, 0–200 m
  6. then the flight computer — which checks the fence once more at START

Measured on the host, feeding mission.py's own output through the C parser:

The parser that faces the outside world is the most attacked code in any
vehicle. A frame with a valid checksum is only undamaged — not sensible.

Slide 23: Five Tasks, Three Locks

TaskPrioPeriodTouches
world510 msworld (world_lock), act (critical section)
ctl420 msworld → sensors_t, flight state (fc_lock)
link3per byteflight state; world via $PARAM,WIND
tel2200 msflight state, then the truth
ui120 msflight state, world (knob); OLED, alone
  • One lock order. Only link ever holds two locks, and always fc_lock then world_lock. One order, no deadlock (lesson 07).
  • Copy, then work. ui copies a view under the lock in microseconds and draws from the copy; the ~25 ms I²C flush runs with no lock held.
  • The actuators are four words, so they cross with __disable_irq() — a mutex would cost more than the copy.
  • The world is highest: the physics must never stall because a display is busy. On a real drone the "world" is reality, and it does not wait either.

Slide 26: What a Step Costs on the Chip

The M0+ has no FPU: every float + and * is a library call. How long is one
50 Hz control step? Time it with the kernel's own SysTick:

static stamp_t stamp(void)                     /* (milliseconds, counter) */
{
    stamp_t s;
    do { s.ms = os_ticks();  s.val = SysTick->VAL; } while (s.ms != os_ticks());
    return s;
}
static uint32_t cycles(stamp_t a, stamp_t b)
{
    return (b.ms - a.ms) * (SysTick->LOAD + 1u) + a.val - b.val;
}

stats prints the last, worst and mean cost of world_step() and
control_step() in µs, plus every task's CPU share. Not yet measured —
this needs the board or Wokwi running. What the code predicts:
control_step() contains 114 calls into the soft-float library (count the
bl __aeabi_f... in its disassembly), at very roughly 50–100 cycles each, so
expect a few hundred µs of its 20 ms budget. Lab Part 1 checks the prediction.

Slide 28: What Carries Forward

  • The cascade and its limits return in every robot: lesson 15's line follower is a cross-track loop; lesson 17's balancer is a cascade whose inner loop is not fast enough to ignore — slide 3's table, at the bottom.
  • The two tiers carry on: lesson 16's Python path planner sends waypoints exactly as mission.py does, and the robot's C follows them.
  • Seen, blind, true. Every autonomous system reports what it believes and is judged on what happened. Log both, as $POS and $TRU do.
  • Failsafes first. Checked every cycle, before anything a human asked for.
  • Measure the budget. 27.4 KB of 32 came from a lesson that did not link at first; the three fixes on slide 25 are tools you will use again.
